## 0. Q3 2026 update (activity update of 2026-10-23)

Nothing in the release changes my numbers. GTT is having a strong year for orders, but the strength was in the first half; the third quarter ran at about the pace my base case already assumes.

Orders. 80 orders in the first nine months, 71 of them LNG carriers. At the half-year it was 65 and 56, so Q3 alone brought 15, all LNG carriers. That's a ~60-a-year pace, which is my base case and well short of the ~93 a year the price needs. My base case has 30 orders in H2 2026, and Q3 delivered half of that. Counting the larger royalties on the ethane carriers, onshore tanks, FSRU and FLNG, the nine months come to roughly 75 LNG-carrier equivalents. It's a very good year, but it's one year.

The point I keep coming back to is that one year isn't the decade. Management's 10-year estimate (c.550 LNG carriers for 2026–2035) hasn't changed. If that total holds, a strong 2026 pulls orders forward rather than adding to them. Spread what's left over 2027–35 and the base case drops to about 52 a year, worth ~€111 a share instead of €129. The good news only adds value if the 10-year estimate goes up. There is a real case for that: 51 Mtpa of new liquefaction FIDs so far this year (37 at the half-year), on top of 84 Mtpa in 2025.

Revenue. Nine-month revenue was €570.1mm (−4.9%), so Q3 was €182.8mm, close to the €184–187mm analysts expected. Guidance was confirmed, not raised: €740–780mm of revenue and €490–530mm of EBITDA. The low end needs only €170mm in Q4, against €203mm in Q4 2025. My model's €737mm for the year sits just under the range, as before.

Two things went slightly the wrong way:

- Ship deliveries that had been running early are going back to their contract dates. That moved less than 2% of nine-month revenue out of the period.
- Management is spending more on the new services business (the LNG Performance Centre) and on digital. It's a small pressure on the 2026 margin, spent on businesses that are still tiny next to the royalties. GTT Marine made €47.4mm in nine months, so my €70mm for the year looks a little high (more like €65mm). That's worth less than €1 a share.

The order book is 299 units worth €1.8bn, against 306 units and €1.85bn in June. The company only publishes the year-by-year revenue schedule with the half-year and annual results, so the model keeps the June schedule.

Valuation at €205. The model's values don't depend on the share price, so bear, base and bull stay at €66, €129 and €172, and the weighted value at €124 (−40%). The reverse DCF now needs about 93 orders a year for nine years, against 96 before. The view stays expensive.

What would change it is the same as before: a higher 10-year estimate at the annual results on 24 February 2027, or two or three years of orders above ~80.

*Why I looked at it: GTT came up in my weekly research reading, and what caught my attention is that it's close to a monopoly. Almost every large LNG carrier uses its tank technology. Monopolies are rare in listed markets, and the obvious question is how much of that the price already includes.*

## 1. Thesis in one sentence

> GTT has one of the best business models in Europe: a near-monopoly on LNG carrier tanks, royalties instead of factories, and a 68% EBITDA margin. But at €212 the market is paying for roughly 95 orders a year for the next decade, while GTT's own estimate works out to about 60.

## 3. The business

GTT doesn't build ships. It licenses the membrane systems that hold LNG at −163°C inside the hull, and it is paid a royalty per vessel. Shipyards in Korea and China pay it in instalments as construction progresses. Almost every large LNG carrier built today uses GTT technology, which explains why EBITDA margins sit near 68%, capex is tiny and about 80% of net income goes out as dividends.

The order book makes revenue unusually visible. At 30 June 2026 it held 306 units, worth €1,853mm of future revenue: €300mm in H2 2026, €655mm in 2027, €603mm in 2028 and €295mm in 2029 and beyond. 2026 guidance is €740–780mm of revenue and €490–530mm of EBITDA.

What visibility doesn't tell you is the size of the next wave. 2025 revenue (€803mm) still reflected the 2022 order peak of 162 units. Orders in 2025 fell to 45, then H1 2026 brought 65.

## 4. Valuation

The model builds revenue from two parts. The first is the order book, using the company's own schedule. The second is future orders × royalty per vessel, recognised over construction. I derived the royalty (€9.5mm per LNG carrier equivalent) and the recognition profile (20% / 55% / 25% over the three years after the order) by comparing the company's order-book schedule in December 2025 and June 2026. The 65 H1 orders added about €596mm.

The near term matches what the company and the Street expect. Modelled 2026 revenue is €737mm (guidance €740–780mm), and 2027 base revenue is ~€810mm against consensus of €815mm.

WACC is 9.6%: French 10-year OAT at 4.75%, beta 0.9, ERP 5.5%.

The reverse DCF is clearer still. At my WACC, the price needs about **96 orders a year for nine years**. Lowering the discount rate helps, but not enough on its own:

To get to €212 you need both a low discount rate (~7.5%) and orders around 75 a year, above management's own estimate. The sell-side mean target of €234 presumably assumes something like that.

Monte Carlo. Across 20,000 simulated DCFs, with orders drawn around the company's ~60 a year and lumpy from year to year ([details](../../valuation/README.md)), the median is €125 and 8.7% of runs exceed €212 (16% with wider distributions). Average order intake explains far more of the value than anything else, including the WACC.

## 5. Bull case (what would make me wrong)

- LNG demand keeps getting revised up. New liquefaction capacity in the US, Qatar and Africa (37 Mtpa of new investment decisions in H1 2026 alone) needs ships. The order wave could be bigger and last longer than the company's 10-year estimate.
- Fleet renewal. Older steam-turbine LNG carriers will need replacing over the next decade, which adds demand that doesn't depend on LNG growth.
- New markets. FLNG, FSRU and large onshore tanks carry bigger royalties per unit, and GTT is winning firsts (the largest GST® onshore tanks, the first US FLNG). GTT Marine (Danelec) adds a digital business.
- My discount rate may be too high. GTT's regression beta is 0.42, and French government yields carry a political risk premium that GTT, paid by Asian shipyards, doesn't really share. At a beta of 0.5 the WACC drops to ~7.5%.

## 6. Bear case (why I think it's expensive)

- The price needs more than the plan. Even at a 7.5% WACC, management's own ~60 orders a year only supports ~€167. The market is paying for an above-plan decade.
- Orders are lumpy. The model can't predict which year is a peak: 162 units in 2022, 45 in 2025. After an ordering wave, a period of fleet overcapacity is the historical norm.
- Competition in China. Chinese yards are a growing share of LNG carrier construction, and domestic membrane designs are the main long-term risk to GTT's share and royalty rates.
- Policy and geopolitics. LNG demand projections depend on energy policy. Even the Middle East disruption the company calls neutral today shows how exposed the chain is.

What would change my view: order intake above ~80 LNG carrier equivalents a year for two or three years; new long-term LNG supply deals that push the company's 10-year estimate up; or the price falling towards ~€150, where my base case at a lower WACC would offer a margin of safety.
